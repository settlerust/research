# Kimi, GLM, and DeepSeek

**GLM 5.3 won six of ten games**, a majority of this Catan competition.
DeepSeek won two; Kimi K3 and **Expert**, an expectimax player using learned
n-tuple tables, won one each. All ten scheduled games reached an engine-recorded
winner. None was excluded or replaced.

<ResearchCompetition asset="llm-arena-expert-competition" />

The three language models run on Baseten. Expert runs locally without an LLM.

GLM finished ahead of Expert in seven games. Its average finish was 2.00 and its
average final score was 8.1 points. The rest of the table resists a simple ranking:
DeepSeek won more than Expert, but Expert had the better average finish
(2.40 versus 2.60) and score (6.7 versus 6.3), and finished ahead of DeepSeek in
six games, with one tie. Kimi averaged third place and 6.0 points, but won the
longest game and finished one point behind the winner in round nine.

The registered hypothesis, that at least one LLM would win more games than
Expert, is supported descriptively. Ten games still leave substantial
uncertainty: GLM's approximate 95% Wilson win-rate interval is 31–83%,
DeepSeek's is 6–51%, and the two one-win players' intervals are 2–40%.
These are finishes at one four-player table, not separate head-to-head matches
or a universal model rating. The [completed-run audit](../records/runs/63ac76cc-e098-4974-8b2f-61a5d3d85be5.md)
records the denominator, checks and limitations.

## What decided games

In [round one](/replay/kimi-glm-deepseek-expert-text-2026-09-17-01), GLM won
without building a city. Four settlements, Longest Road, Largest Army and two
hidden victory cards made ten points. On turn 68 it played its third knight,
stole grain from DeepSeek, exchanged four grain for brick and built the winning
settlement. This was a win assembled from several scoring routes rather than
one large production engine.

[Round nine](/replay/kimi-glm-deepseek-expert-text-2026-09-17-09) shows
DeepSeek turning trade history into a persistent preference. GLM supplied it
with grain for brick on turn 25, for ore on turn 37 and for wool on turn 41.
DeepSeek's scratchpad began saying “Trust seat2” while remembering Expert's
rejections. It then finished with a city upgrade on turn 61, beating Kimi 10–9.
The trades and private preference are recorded; they do not establish a mutual
alliance or show that trusting GLM caused the win.

In [round ten](/replay/kimi-glm-deepseek-expert-text-2026-09-17-10), Expert's
Monopoly took five wool from Kimi on turn 36. Kimi subsequently called Expert
“hostile” in its scratchpad. The competition later became a road race: Expert
took Longest Road on turn 80 and Kimi reclaimed it on turn 81. Kimi upgraded a
city on turn 89 and built the winning settlement on turn 101, finishing 10–9
over DeepSeek. Its notebook changed from trusting an early trade partner to
tracking resource shortages, road threats and opponents refusing late offers.

Expert's [round-four win](/replay/kimi-glm-deepseek-expert-text-2026-09-17-04)
came from two cities, one settlement, Largest Army and three victory cards.
Buying the last development card ended the game on turn 69. The baseline also
finished on nine points in two losses, so its single win understates how often
it remained competitive.

## Cooperation was mostly transactional

The table completed **84 trades from 463 offers**, about 18%. Another 181
offers expired and 45 closed when the turn ended. These are distinct offers,
not one count per recipient. Trading was a substantial form of cooperation,
but most proposals did not become exchanges.

There were only 25 standalone chat messages: 23 from DeepSeek and two from
Kimi. This excludes text attached to trade offers. Kimi explicitly appealed to
leader containment in round eight, asking Expert for lumber because GLM held
both awards. DeepSeek mostly explained how a proposed exchange would fund its
next build. GLM sent no standalone chat while participating in 53 completed
trades, the most of any player. Each trade counts for both participants.

The clearest social memory was in private notes, not sustained conversation.
DeepSeek recorded 131 scratchpad updates, Kimi 97 and GLM 48. The reviewed
examples show remembered help and grudges, but do not demonstrate a durable
alliance or coordinated embargo.

## Acting before the deadline

The same two-minute turn ceiling produced very different behavior.

| Player | Reported generated tokens | Active request errors | Engine timeouts |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| GLM 5.3 | 87,091 | 6 | 5 |
| DeepSeek | 1,904,657 | 104 | 110 |
| Kimi K3 | 202,156 | 51 | 43 |
| Expert | 0 | 0 | 5 |

Generated tokens include returned reasoning, off-turn responses and successful
compaction responses. They exclude input tokens and unreported work from failed
requests. Active request errors exclude intentional cancellations and off-turn
errors; an engine timeout is a separate recorded rule event. These columns
overlap and should not be added together. Off-turn request errors were also
common: 103 for GLM, 123 for DeepSeek and 124 for Kimi.

DeepSeek generated about 22 times as many tokens as GLM. One response on turn
31 of round ten spent 101 seconds and all 16,384 output tokens repeatedly
trying to interpret board adjacency, ending at the output limit without a tool
call. The text map did not eliminate spatial confusion. Across the series,
15 of its active decision responses hit an output limit. Extra reasoning was
not consistently converted into a move.

Service failures also matter. Kimi recorded 48 HTTP 500 errors on decision
requests, versus four for DeepSeek and one for GLM. It would be misleading to
attribute every missed move to strategy. Tool receipts recorded 73 errors in
total, including 37 expired activations and ten aborted operations; only five
were schema validation failures, all from DeepSeek. Legal-action failures and
trade races account for other errors. These are different failure modes.

GLM combined the most wins with the fewest generated tokens and few engine
timeouts in this run. That is useful evidence for this timed configuration,
but does not isolate model strategy from inference latency, tool reliability,
input modality or the shared search advice.

## The comparison

Each round used a distinct board and a fresh private memory for each player.
Starting positions are counterbalanced in four-game blocks, so each entrant
starts in each position two or three times across the ten games. Two games ran
at once. The stop rule retained any failed or incomplete attempt and stopped
new launches for investigation; no match in this series triggered it.

Wins are the primary outcome. Places rank the engine winner first, then the
revealed final scores, with average ranks for ties. The win-rate intervals
assume independent games under these conditions. Ten different boards with
counterbalanced starts do not provide a paired comparison of every model on
every board and seat.

Expert uses depth-3 expectimax, a four-second search budget, a 1.2-million-node
ceiling and the frozen learned tables. It chooses the highest-ranked legal
command, including trade decisions. It evaluates offers but does not interpret
chat. The LLMs receive automatic search advice and can request additional
analysis, including a sampled view of an opponent’s incentives.

## Playing through tools

The open-source arena is a self-contained TypeScript harness around the Rust
rules engine. Each LLM sees its hand, public scores and card counts, bank supply,
legal actions and live offers as labelled text. A fixed ASCII hex map shows
tiles, intersections and roads using the same identifiers as tool calls. Search
rankings describe nearby resources and road endpoints, and resource counts name
their resource. Tool receipts, scratchpad and conversation memory are text too. Kimi and GLM also receive a board image; DeepSeek receives text.
No player receives an opponent’s private hand, scratchpad or future randomness.

An active turn allows two minutes and up to 50,000 generated tokens, including
reasoning, across at most 24 requests. Each response can generate up to 16,384
tokens. Input context is reported separately. Each tool receipt refreshes the
world state and reports the remaining time and token allowance.

| Tool | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| `play_action` | Build, play cards, move the robber, or propose, accept, reject and counter trades |
| `analyze` | Rank actions or examine another player’s likely incentives under a chosen search budget |
| `chat` | Negotiate publicly or privately, choose which conversations to follow, and read the inbox |
| `scratchpad` | Replace one private planning document, automatically reloaded on the next decision |

The harness rolls automatically when no knight is playable. With a playable
knight, the agent chooses whether to use it before rolling. Returning without
more tool calls ends the activation. The Rust engine resolves mandatory
unfinished decisions at the deadline.

Players can hold side conversations and respond to offers during other turns.
Off-turn work has a separate 2,000-token allowance and yields when their own
decision starts. Limits on messages, characters and recipients prevent one
player from exhausting another’s attention. Conversation history persists
across turns and compacts after 20 decision cycles or 60,000 historical
characters, retaining up to four recent exchanges. The scratchpad survives
compaction and can hold plans, promises, alliances and grudges.

## Inspecting a match

Each round opens in the normal full-screen 3D game viewer. **Timeline** shows
public moves and conversation; **Spectate** shows each agent’s returned
reasoning, the actions between responses and its scratchpad at the selected
time. Private activity is marked “Spectators only.” The bottom playback track
supports seeking, play/pause and speed changes.

Browser recordings preserve this sequence while omitting repeated model input
prompts, embedded input images and duplicate provider payloads. Full research
archives are retained separately with verified hashes. The ten browser files
total 17.5 MB, versus 638.6 MB for the full source Parquet files, a 97% reduction.
Request cards show time
and reported input/output usage; reasoning tokens are part of output, not an
extra charge added twice. Missing provider reasoning is never reconstructed.

## Why Inkling was replaced

Inkling was tested in the earlier competition, but repeatedly failed to call
tools correctly and accepted poor trades. This gave opponents willing to trade
with it an unfair advantage and made the table unsuitable for a useful strength
comparison. We retired that competition and replaced Inkling with Expert. Its
original recordings, report and source metadata remain archived for inspection.
